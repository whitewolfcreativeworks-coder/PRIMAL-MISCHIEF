# Historical notes and representation

Primal Mischief draws on real civilizations. Card mechanics are
*inspired by* documented strengths; they are game abstractions, not
claims that any society was "better" at anything. This file records what
each civ's design rests on and where we took artistic licence.

General principles followed:

- Civilizations are represented by institutions, technology, economy and
  architecture — not only by warfare.
- No caricature, no "savage/civilized" framing, no ethnic visual
  stereotypes. Human figures are deliberately simplified silhouettes.
- Religious subjects appear only as architecture (temples, a lion-temple
  gateway). No sacred iconography is reproduced.
- Flavor text uses hedged language ("attest", "about", "c.") where
  evidence is approximate.
- All art is procedural, original SVG; it is **artistic interpretation**,
  not reconstruction.

## Kingdom of Kush (Nile Valley, Sudan; c. 785 BCE – 350 CE)

- Capitals at Napata and later Meroë; Kushite kings ruled Egypt as the
  25th Dynasty (c. 744–656 BCE).
- Meroë has large iron-slag deposits indicating substantial iron
  production; the *scale* is debated by archaeologists, hence the card's
  cautious wording.
- Egyptian sources called the region *Ta-Seti*, "Land of the Bow",
  reflecting a long archery tradition.
- More than 200 royal pyramids survive (Meroë, Nuri, el-Kurru); they are
  smaller and steeper than Old Kingdom Egyptian pyramids, with offering
  chapels — reflected in the `pyramid` motif.
- *Kandake* (Greek *Candace*) was a title of ruling queens/queen mothers.
  Amanirenas fought Roman forces c. 25–21 BCE.
- Apedemak was a lion-headed deity; the Lion Temple at Naqa depicts him.
  The card represents the temple building only.

## Kingdom of Aksum (Ethiopian & Eritrean highlands; c. 100 – 940 CE)

- Major Red Sea power trading with Rome, Arabia and India via the port of
  Adulis.
- Issued its own gold, silver and bronze coinage from roughly the reign of
  Endubis (late 3rd century CE) — among the earliest African states to do so.
- Monumental carved granite stelae mark royal tombs; the largest (fallen)
  was over 30 m; the standing "King Ezana's Stele" is about 21 m. The card
  says "some over 20 m", which is safe.
- Aksum adopted Christianity in the 4th century; we deliberately do not
  turn religion into a mechanic.

## Carthage (North African coast, Tunisia; c. 814 – 146 BCE)

- Traditional foundation date 814 BCE by settlers from Tyre (the date comes
  from later Greek/Roman authors; archaeology supports the late 9th–8th
  century).
- The *cothon*: an enclosed circular naval harbour with a central island
  and ship sheds, excavated in Carthage.
- Relied heavily on mercenaries and allied troops (Iberian, Gallic,
  Balearic, Numidian, Libyan). The *Wage* mechanic models that dependence,
  including the risk of unpaid troops — the Mercenary War (241–237 BCE)
  began over pay.
- Numidian light cavalry fought for (and later against) Carthage.
- Most surviving accounts are by Carthage's enemies (Greek and Roman),
  which we keep in mind: Carthage is a trading republic here, not a
  villain.

## Sumer (Southern Mesopotamia, Iraq; c. 4500 – 1900 BCE)

- City-states such as Uruk, Ur, Lagash; among the earliest cities.
- Irrigation canals from the Tigris and Euphrates enabled agriculture.
- Ziggurats were stepped temple platforms of mud brick (e.g. Ur,
  c. 21st century BCE — technically Neo-Sumerian).
- Cuneiform began largely as administrative record-keeping, which is why
  Sumer's card-draw engine is a *Tablet House*.
- The Standard of Ur (c. 2500 BCE) shows four-wheeled battle wagons drawn
  by equids (likely onager hybrids).
- Uruk's walls are about 9 km long; the Epic of Gilgamesh credits him with
  building them — flagged as legend on the card.

## Inca (Tawantinsuyu, Andes; c. 1438 – 1533 CE)

- The Qhapaq Ñan road system: estimates range roughly 30,000–40,000 km; the
  card says "roughly 30,000 km".
- *Chasqui* relay runners carried messages and khipu (knotted-cord
  records).
- Terraces (*andenes*) and storehouses (*qullqa*) made the state resilient
  to bad harvests — hence famine immunity.
- Fitted ashlar masonry without mortar resists earthquakes.
- The sling (*warak'a*) was a common weapon.
- The Inca are later than the other four; the game is "historical", not
  strictly "ancient", and includes them for South American representation
  and a strongly distinct play style.

## Expansion roster (not yet implemented)

Ancient Egypt, Kerma, Nok culture, Assyrian Empire, Hittite Empire,
Achaemenid Persia, Phoenician city-states, Indus Valley Civilization, Han
China, Khmer Empire, Ancient Greece, Ancient Rome, Norse societies, Maya,
Olmec, Mississippian cultures, Moche. Notes from the original brief:

- **Nok**: evidence is mainly terracotta sculpture and early iron smelting;
  little is known about political organization. Any Nok design should be
  craft/metallurgy-focused and say clearly what is interpretation.
- **Kerma**: notable for the Western Deffufa and early urban Nubia; pairs
  naturally with Kush as an earlier Nubian power.
- **Indus Valley**: script undeciphered; avoid inventing rulers or
  religion. Lean on urban planning, drainage, standardized weights.
- **Mississippian**: earthwork mounds (Cahokia); consult descendant
  communities' public guidance where possible.
